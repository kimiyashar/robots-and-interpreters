Topic 1 of 2

Robotics

Four ideas: how a robot's brain is organized, two ways to control its motors, the hardware behind the better way, and the problems that kept robots clumsy for decades.

1 · THE CORE LOOP

Sense, Think, Act

Almost every robot runs the same three part loop, over and over, many times per second. This way of organizing a robot is called the Sense, Think, Act paradigm.

Sense
Gather any new information. The notes list three kinds: the environment (cameras, distance sensors, touch sensors), the robot's own state (joint angles, speed, battery level), and external commands (a person's joystick, a message from another computer).
Think
Use that information to draw conclusions or make estimates, then plan an action. Example: "the wall is 20 cm ahead and I am moving fast, so I should turn left now."
Act
Carry out the plan by moving motors, sending messages, or controlling other outputs such as lights or a speaker.

Why the notes compare it to MVC

In 15-112 graphics apps you already use Model, View, Controller. The model is the data stored in app. The controller is your event functions, like onKeyPress and onStep, which decide how that data changes. The view is redrawAll, which shows the result.

  • Sense is like updating the model. New sensor readings get stored as the robot's current picture of itself and the world.
  • Think is like the controller. It looks at the model and decides what should happen next.
  • Act is like the view. It is the visible output. Instead of drawing on a screen, the robot "draws" by moving in the real world.

A quick example: a robot vacuum senses that its bump sensor was pressed, thinks "I hit something, I should back up and turn," and acts by spinning its wheels. Then it senses again.

2 · CONTROLLING MOTORS

Position control versus force control

When a robot moves a joint, there are two very different things you can tell the motor to do.

Position controlForce control
You commandWhere the joint should be: "go to 45 degrees and stay there."How hard the joint should push, or how stiff it should feel.
If something is in the wayIt pushes as hard as it needs to reach the target anyway.It can feel the contact and give way, like your arm does when someone bumps it.
Best forExtremely fast, repetitive motion in predictable places, like a factory arm welding the same spot on every car.Moving more like a human: reacting to touch and recovering from unexpected collisions.
Typical lookOlder walking robots: stable, but slow, stiff and toy like.Newer robots that walk, run and catch themselves more naturally.

How older walking robots stayed upright

Older walkers used position control and followed one strict rule: keep the center of mass (or the zero moment point) above the support polygon at all times. Three terms to know:

Center of mass
The average position of all the robot's mass. It is the point the robot balances around.
Support polygon
Look down from above and stretch a rubber band around every part of the robot touching the ground. The shape inside is the support polygon. With both feet down it is large. Mid step, on one foot, it shrinks to just that foot.
Zero moment point (ZMP)
The point on the ground where the combined effect of gravity and the robot's motion produces no tipping. It is the moving version of the center of mass idea: if the ZMP stays inside the support polygon, the robot does not tip over.

The hardware that makes force control work

A traditional motor uses lots of gearing to multiply its strength. Heavy gearing adds friction and makes the joint stiff, so the motor cannot easily feel what is pushing back on it. That is a poor fit for force control. The notes name better options:

  • Series elastic actuators. A spring is placed in series (in a chain) between the motor's gearbox and the joint. Measure how far the spring stretches, and you know the force, because for a spring force equals stiffness times stretch (F = k · x, Hooke's law). The spring also absorbs shocks.
  • Compliant joints. Joints designed to give a little instead of being perfectly rigid.
  • Very high torque direct drive motors with less gearing. This is what many more modern robots use. With little gearing there is less friction, so the motor itself can sense and control force well.

3 · WHY THIS WAS HARD

Limitations in robotics

The notes list what held robots back in the past and what still holds them back now. Learn these as a list. They are easy exam questions.

  1. Until the late 2010s

    Computing and control algorithms

    Fast, reliable computing hardware and good control algorithms were missing. Dynamic motion (running, jumping, catching your balance) needs very fast, correct decisions, so it stayed limited.

  2. Until about 2020

    Battery and motor power density

    Power density means how much power you get per kilogram. A humanoid robot has to carry its own batteries and motors, so weak power density was a barrier to practical, agile humanoids.

  3. Still true

    Human safety

    Dynamic robots often fail in unexpected ways. A common misconception: compliant (springy) joints do not necessarily make a collision with a person less dangerous.

  4. Today's barriers

    Perception, battery capacity, compact compute

    Reliably perceiving and interpreting the environment. Battery capacity for long runtimes. Compact computers powerful enough to run higher level planning with LLMs on the robot itself.

Do not mix these up

Power density (power per kilogram) was the older barrier for agile humanoids. Battery capacity (how long you can run) is listed as a modern barrier. Similar words, different points.

Writing an interpreter for Lisp

In lecture, a working Lisp interpreter was written in about an hour, in Python, using only things you already know: strings, lists, dictionaries and recursion. Here is every idea behind it.

1 · THE LANGUAGE

What is Lisp?

Lisp is a real programming language, invented by John McCarthy at MIT in 1958. It is the second oldest programming language still in use today. Only Fortran is older.

Lecture built a small subset of Lisp and changed it a little. For example, it uses a command called block where real Lisp uses progn. Both mean "run these expressions in order and give back the last one."

One idea to hold onto for the whole topic: code is just a multiline string of text. Lisp code, Python code, all code. An interpreter is simply a program that reads that string and does what it says.

2 · READING LISP

Prefix notation

Python puts operators between values: 1 + 2 + 3. Lisp puts the command first, inside parentheses:

(command arg0 arg1 ... argN)

(+ 1 2 3)        ? adds 1, 2 and 3 to get 6

Every expression has that shape. Because an argument can be another expression, expressions nest. Here is the example from the notes, solved from the inside out:

StepExpressionWhat happened
Start(+ (+ 1 2) (* 3 4) (+ 5 (* 6 7)))The outer + has three arguments, all expressions.
1(+ 3 12 (+ 5 42))(+ 1 2) is 3, (* 3 4) is 12, (* 6 7) is 42.
2(+ 3 12 47)(+ 5 42) is 47.
3623 + 12 + 47.
A wording detail

The notes call this "prefix or RPN notation." Strictly speaking, Lisp uses prefix notation (also called Polish notation), where the command comes first. RPN stands for Reverse Polish Notation, where the operator comes last (1 2 +). For the exam, the key fact is that in Lisp the command comes first.

3 · THE BIG PICTURE

Interpreter versus compiler

Interpreter
A program that takes code as input and directly runs it. This is what lecture wrote.
Compiler
A program that translates code into another language, such as assembly language, which then gets run later.

The lecture interpreter works in three steps, always in this order:

4 · STEP 1

Tokenize: string to list of tokens

Tokenizing starts with the code (one big string) and returns a 1D list of tokens. A token is the smallest meaningful piece of the language, like a word in a sentence. It can be:

  • a keyword, like while
  • a variable, like x
  • an integer, like 123 (stored as a real Python int, not the string "123")
  • legal punctuation, like a parenthesis
  • any other legal piece of syntax in the language

Tokenizing is also where whitespace and comments are removed. After this step, the interpreter never sees a space or a comment again.

Lecture added a special EOF (End Of File) character to the end of the input to make tokenizing easier, so the loop always knows when to stop. It used chr(0), because chr(0) can never appear in real code.

For the notes' example, the tokens are:

( parenthesis + punctuation x name 12 integer EOF end marker, chr(0)
5 · STEP 2

Parse: list of tokens to parse tree

A flat list does not show which pieces are inside which. Parsing fixes that. It takes the 1D list of tokens and returns a parse tree: a hierarchical data structure that captures the structure of the program, so you can tell which parts are structurally within other parts.

To keep things simple and use only what 112 has covered, lecture represented the parse tree with multidimensional lists. Each expression becomes a list, and sub expressions become lists inside lists.

['+',
    ['+', 1, 2],
    ['*', 3, 4],
    ['+', 5,
          ['*', 6, 7]
    ]
]

Parsing uses recursion, because expressions can be arbitrarily nested: an expression can contain an expression, which contains another, and so on. Each time the parser sees (, it calls itself to parse each argument, and each of those calls can do the same thing. A loop alone cannot easily handle unlimited depth. Recursion handles it naturally.

6 · STEP 3

Evaluate: run the tree

Evaluating is where the code finally runs. It takes the parse tree and performs the computations that produce the program's output.

It also uses recursion, for the same reason as parsing: expressions can be nested to any depth. To evaluate ['+', ['+', 1, 2], 4], the evaluator first evaluates each argument (calling itself on ['+', 1, 2] to get 3), then adds the results.

Where variables and functions live

Variables: a dictionary
A dictionary maps variable names to their values. At the top level there is one dictionary for the globals.
Local variables: a new dictionary per call
Just before calling a user defined function, the interpreter creates a brand new dictionary to hold that function's local variables (starting with its parameters).
Functions: another dictionary
A second dictionary maps each function name to a tuple of its parameter list and its body. The parameter list is a list of names, like ['x', 'y']. The body is a parse tree of the code to run when the function is called.
# after running (function f (x y) (+ x y))
functionsMap = { 'f': ( ['x', 'y'], ['+', 'x', 'y'] ) }
#                      parameters    body (a parse tree)

Errors

The interpreter raised exceptions when it detected common mistakes, such as illegal tokens, undefined variables, and unknown commands. The notes point out this was extremely helpful for debugging in real time while writing the interpreter.

7 · HONEST TRADEOFFS

Shortcuts lecture took

To finish in just over an hour using mostly 112 material, lecture cut corners. The one the notes highlight: the tokenizer stored the code in a list and popped off the front with pop(0). That is very inefficient and should not be done in practice.

Why: removing the first item of a Python list forces every other item to shift one spot left. For a list of length n, that is O(n) work, every single time. Do it for all n characters and the tokenizer becomes O(n²).

The notes list three better ways:

FixHow it worksCost per step
Reverse, then pop from the rightReverse the list once, then use pop(), which removes the last item without shifting anything.O(1)
Use a dequeA deque is like a list but is built to pop off the front efficiently, with popleft().O(1)
Use indexesNever change the list. Keep an index i and move it forward.O(1)

The annotated Python version on this site uses the index approach.

8 · THE FINAL TEST

Running recursive Fibonacci

Lecture tested the finished interpreter by running a recursive Fibonacci function written in Lisp. It worked. Read it with the commands you now know:

(block
    (function fib (n)            ? define fib with one parameter, n
        (if (< n 2)               ? if n < 2 ...
            1                    ? ... then the answer is 1
            (+ (fib (- n 1))     ? ... else fib(n-1) + fib(n-2)
               (fib (- n 2))
            )
        )
    )
    (set x 0)                    ? x = 0
    (while (< x 10)              ? while x < 10:
        (block
            (output x (fib x))   ?     print x and fib(x)
            (set  x (+ x 1))     ?     x += 1
        )
    )
)

It prints [0, 1], [1, 1], [2, 2], [3, 3], [4, 5], and so on up to [9, 55]. Note that this version of fib starts 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, so fib(0) is 1.

Open the Playground and pick "Fibonacci" to watch every one of its steps.

A compiler translates it to another language. 3.
